Chapter 1

Atoms

All matter is made of extremely small particles, invisible to the naked eye, called atoms.

1.1Basic laws and Dalton's atomic theory

Law of conservation of mass

In a chemical reaction, the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.

Law of definite proportions

A pure compound always contains its elements in a fixed ratio by mass. E.g. water: H 11.11% and O 88.89%.

Law of multiple proportions

When two elements form several compounds, their mass ratios are small whole numbers. E.g. CO and CO₂ → 1 : 2.
Dalton's atomic theory (1803)
  • Matter is made of tiny particles called atoms.
  • Atoms of the same element have the same mass, size and properties.
  • Atoms cannot be split, created or destroyed.
  • Atoms of different elements combine in small whole-number ratios.
  • In a chemical reaction, atoms are only rearranged.

Modern theory: the discovery of the electron, X-rays and radioactivity showed that atoms can be split into even smaller particles.

1.2Particles that make up the atom

Protons and neutrons are in the nucleus. Electrons are very light, so almost all of an atom's mass is in its nucleus: \(m_{\text{atom}}\approx m_p+m_n\).

1.3Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same \(Z\) but a different number of neutrons (and so a different \(A\)). They have the same chemical properties.

Average atomic mass \[\bar M(\mathrm{Cl})=35\times\frac{75.76}{100}+37\times\frac{24.24}{100}\approx35.5\,\text{u}\]
Multiply the mass of each isotope by its natural abundance (%) and add the results.

1.4Electron shells

Electrons are arranged in shells \(K, L, M, N,\dots\) (\(n=1,2,3,4\)) going outward from the nucleus. Inner shells fill first. Shell \(n\) holds at most:

Maximum number of electrons \[N_{\max}=2n^2\qquad K\,(n{=}1):2\quad L\,(n{=}2):8\quad M\,(n{=}3):18\]

For the first 20 elements, shell \(M\) takes only 8 electrons before shell \(N\) starts. Electrons in the outermost shell are called valence electrons; they decide the chemical properties of the element.

Summary: electrons around the nucleus

  • Attraction: the positive nucleus attracts negative electrons. A neutral atom has Z protons and Z electrons, so its total charge is zero.
  • Shells and energy: electrons occupy allowed energy states around the nucleus. In the shell model, K, L, M… represent successive main energy levels; outer-shell electrons generally have higher energy and are less tightly bound than inner-shell electrons.
  • Changing energy: an electron can absorb energy and move to a higher allowed level. When it returns to a lower level, it releases the energy difference as light. Removing it completely requires enough energy to overcome its attraction to the nucleus.
  • Sodium example: a neutral sodium-23 atom has 11 protons, 12 neutrons and 11 electrons arranged 2,8,1. The single electron in its third shell is its valence electron. Losing it forms Na⁺ with arrangement 2,8.

Atoms: textbook practice

Adapted from textbook printed pages 23–24; worked solutions added for this website.

Write your own answer first. Click a question to reveal the worked solution.

1.Name the positively charged part of an atom. Which particles are inside it? Compare the charges of the three subatomic particles.

Worked solution

The nucleus contains protons and neutrons. A proton has charge +1, a neutron 0 and an electron −1 (in elementary-charge units). Electrons occupy the region around the nucleus. The nucleus is positive because neutrons add no charge.

2.A neutral atom has 3 protons and mass number 7. Find Z, A, its electrons and its neutrons. Identify the element.

Worked solution

\(Z=3\), \(A=7\). Neutrality gives \(e^-=3\). The neutron number is \(N=A-Z=7-3=4\). The element is lithium: \(\ce{^{7}_{3}Li}\).

3.Define isotopes. Is “isotopes of one element have different proton numbers” true or false? Explain.

Worked solution

False. Isotopes have the same proton number Z but different neutron numbers, so their mass numbers differ. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have 6 protons, but have 6 and 8 neutrons respectively.

4.What is the maximum number of electrons in shell M? Show the rule used. Does this mean every atom with three occupied shells has a full M shell?

Worked solution

Shell M has \(n=3\). Its capacity is \(2n^2=2(3)^2=18\) electrons. No: this is a maximum capacity, not the number actually present. Sodium, for example, has arrangement 2,8,1.

5.A neutral atom has electron arrangement 2,8. A student says its atomic number is 8. Correct the statement and explain.

Worked solution

Total electrons \(=2+8=10\). For a neutral atom, protons = electrons, hence \(Z=10\), not 8. It is neon. The number 8 counts only the outer-shell electrons.

6.How many atoms are in 1 mol of iron? Find the mass of 0.20 mol of iron. Use M(Fe) = 56 g/mol and Avogadro’s constant 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

Worked solution

One mole contains \(6.02\times10^{23}\) iron atoms.

\[m=nM=0.20\times56=11.2\,\text{g}\]

Multiply moles by molar mass; dividing would give the wrong units.

Chapter 2

The Periodic Table

The Russian scientist Dmitri Mendeleev (1834–1907) arranged the elements in a table. Today the elements are ordered by increasing atomic number \(Z\).

2.1Groups and periods

Group = column

Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons, so they have similar chemical properties. Group number (I–VIII) = number of valence electrons.

Period = row

Elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells. Period number = number of shells. From left to right, properties change from metal to non-metal.
What are valence electrons?
Valence electrons are the electrons in an atom's outermost shell. They are the electrons that take part in chemical reactions and bonding, so they decide an element's chemical properties. How to find them: write the electron arrangement and look at the last number.

  • Metals (1–3 valence e⁻) easily lose electrons → positive ions (Na⁺) · non-metals (5–7) gain or share electrons → negative ions (Cl⁻) or covalent bonds · noble gases (8; He has 2) hardly react.

2.2Group I: alkali metals

LiNaKRbCsFr have 1 valence electron, so they lose it easily and become \(\mathrm{M^+}\) ions.

  • Soft (can be cut with a knife), low density and low melting point
  • Very reactive → stored in paraffin oil
  • Reactivity increases down the group (Li < Na < K < Rb < Cs) — Why? See explanation
  • Flame colours: Li red · Na yellow · K lilac
With water → base + hydrogen\[\ce{2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g)}\]
With chlorine → salt\[\ce{2Na(s) + Cl2(g) -> 2NaCl(s)}\]
With oxygen → oxide\[\ce{4Na(s) + O2(g) -> 2Na2O(s)}\]

Why does reactivity increase down Group I?

Recall the positive nucleus and negative electrons (§1.2) and electron shells and valence electrons (§1.4). Alkali metals react by losing their single outer electron to form M⁺.

  1. More occupied shells: Li has arrangement 2,1; Na has 2,8,1; K has 2,8,8,1. Down the group, the outer electron is farther from the nucleus.
  2. More shielding: inner electrons reduce the attraction felt by the outer electron from the positive nucleus. Although nuclear charge increases, greater distance and shielding dominate for the outer electron down this group.
  3. Easier electron loss: less energy is needed to remove that electron (lower first ionisation energy). The metal therefore reacts more readily.

More shells → greater distance and shielding → easier loss of the outer electron → greater reactivity. Thus Li < Na < K < Rb < Cs. Under comparable conditions, potassium reacts with water more vigorously than sodium, and sodium more vigorously than lithium.

This explanation concerns Group I metals, which lose electrons. It should not be applied directly to halogens, which gain electrons.

FClBrI have 7 valence electrons. They are very reactive non-metals that gain 1 electron to become \(\mathrm{X^-}\) ions. Their molecules have 2 atoms: \(\mathrm{F_2, Cl_2, Br_2, I_2}\). Reactivity decreases down the group.

2.4Group VIII: noble gases

HeNeArKrXeRn have a full outer shell (8 electrons; He has 2). They are very stable, hardly react at all, and exist as single atoms.

Uses

He fills balloons and airships · Ne advertising lights · Ar fills light bulbs

2.5Transition metals

Elements in the middle block of the table (Fe, Cu, Cr, Ni, Zn …).

Physical

Hard, with high density and high melting points (e.g. Fe 1535 °C). Good conductors of heat and electricity.

Variable valency

\(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) / \(\mathrm{Fe^{3+}}\), \(\mathrm{Cu^{+}}\) / \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\). Coloured compounds: \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) blue, \(\mathrm{Fe^{2+}}\) green.

Catalysts

Fe in making ammonia (Haber process) · \(\mathrm{V_2O_5}\) in making sulfuric acid.

Periodic table: textbook practice

Adapted from textbook printed pages 48–49; worked solutions added for this website.

Write your own answer first. Click a question to reveal the worked solution.

1.What quantity determines the order of elements in the modern periodic table: number of shells, proton number, reactivity, or atomic mass?

Worked solution

Increasing proton number (atomic number Z). Each next element has one more proton. The period indicates the occupied electron-shell count for ground-state atoms.

2.Which set of atomic numbers belongs to alkali metals: (7,23,39), (12,20,38), (18,36,54), or (19,37,55)? Name the correct elements.

Worked solution

19,37,55: potassium K, rubidium Rb and caesium Cs. All are in Group I (modern Group 1), with one outer electron and a tendency to form +1 ions.

3.Choose the Group I element from Mg, Fe, Cu and K. Write its ion and explain how that ion forms.

Worked solution

Potassium K. It loses one outer electron:

\[\ce{K -> K+ + e-}\]

Mg is in Group II; Fe and Cu are in the central metal block.

4.State how halogen colour, physical state and reactivity change down Group VII. Does reactivity increase down this group?

Worked solution

Colour generally becomes darker. At room temperature, F₂ and Cl₂ are gases, Br₂ is a liquid and I₂ is a solid. Reactivity decreases down the group: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂.

5.Element X is in Group VII below chlorine. Which statement is false: X displaces chlorine from KCl; X has diatomic molecules; X reacts with sodium to form an ionic compound? Explain.

Worked solution

“X displaces chlorine from KCl” is false. X is less reactive than chlorine, so cannot displace it. The other two statements are true for bromine or iodine. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from its halide.

6.A student says “a xenon molecule has two atoms and xenon burns easily.” Correct both errors and explain why xenon is relatively unreactive.

Worked solution

Xenon exists as single atoms, Xe, and is non-flammable. Its full outer shell makes it relatively unreactive. “Relatively unreactive” does not mean it can never form compounds under special conditions.

7.Which formula for a barium compound is incorrect: BaCl₂, Ba(OH)₂, BaNO₃, BaSO₄? Correct it using ionic charges.

Worked solution

BaNO₃ is incorrect. Barium is \(\ce{Ba^{2+}}\); nitrate is \(\ce{NO3-}\). Two nitrate ions are needed to balance +2, so the correct formula is \(\ce{Ba(NO3)2}\). The other formulas are electrically neutral.

8.X, Y and Z are in the same period. X is a metal, Y a non-metal and Z a noble gas. Put them in order from left to right and justify your answer.

Worked solution

X → Y → Z. Metals occupy the left side, non-metals the right side, and noble gases the last group of a period. For example: Na → Cl → Ar.

Transition metals: ability check

Additional teacher practice based on this lesson.

Write your own answer first. Click a question to reveal the worked solution.

1.Give two physical properties that make copper useful for electrical wires. Explain each choice.

Worked solution

Good electrical conductivity: mobile electrons carry charge through the metal. Ductility: copper can be drawn into long, thin wires without breaking.

2.Iron forms Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ ions. Write the formulas of iron(II) chloride and iron(III) chloride. Why are the formulas different?

Worked solution

Chloride has charge −1. Charge balance gives \(\ce{FeCl2}\) for Fe²⁺ and \(\ce{FeCl3}\) for Fe³⁺. Iron has variable oxidation states, so different numbers of chloride ions are needed.

3.What is a catalyst? Name the catalyst used in the Haber process. Is it used up overall in the reaction?

Worked solution

A catalyst increases reaction rate by providing a pathway with lower activation energy. Iron catalyses ammonia production in the Haber process. It participates in reaction steps but is regenerated, so is not consumed overall.

4.A pale green solution may contain Fe²⁺; a blue solution may contain Cu²⁺. Is colour alone sufficient to prove which ion is present? Explain.

Worked solution

No. These are typical colours of hydrated ions in water, but other substances can have similar colours, and concentration changes colour intensity. Colour is a clue; identification needs an additional chemical test.

Chapter 3

Chemical Bonding and Solid Structures

Octet rule
Atoms tend to give, take or share electrons so that their outer shell has 8 electrons (like a noble gas), or 2 for H (like He).

3.1Covalent bonds (sharing electrons)

Form between non-metals and non-metals. The atoms share 1 pair of electrons (single bond), 2 pairs (double bond) or 3 pairs (triple bond).

Note
An atom with 7 valence electrons needs 1 shared pair (Cl–Cl); 6 → 2 pairs (O=O); 5 → 3 pairs (N≡N).
An attraction between H and O, N or F on different molecules is called a hydrogen bond (e.g. between water molecules).

3.2Ionic bonds (transferring electrons)

Form between metals (Groups I, II) and non-metals (Groups VI, VII). The metal transfers electrons to the non-metal, making oppositely charged ions that attract each other by electrostatic force.

\[\ce{Na -> Na+ + e-}\qquad\qquad \ce{Cl + e- -> Cl-}\qquad\qquad \ce{Na+ + Cl- -> NaCl}\]

Examples of ionic compounds: \(\mathrm{NaCl}\) (salt), \(\mathrm{CaO}\) (quicklime), \(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\) (limestone), \(\mathrm{MgO}\), \(\mathrm{KF}\), \(\mathrm{NaHCO_3}\) (baking soda).

Bonding and solids: textbook practice

Adapted from textbook printed pages 73–74; worked solutions added for this website.

Write your own answer first. Click a question to reveal the worked solution.

1.Explain the difference between ionic and covalent bonding in terms of electrons. Give one example of each.

Worked solution

Ionic: electron transfer forms positive and negative ions; electrostatic attraction holds them together, as in NaCl. Covalent: atoms share electron pairs, as in H₂. Transfer alone is not the bond; the attraction between ions is.

2.A particle has 20 protons, 20 neutrons and 18 electrons. Identify it, calculate its charge and write its symbol.

Worked solution

\(Z=20\) identifies calcium. \(A=20+20=40\); charge \(=20-18=+2\). Therefore it is a calcium cation, \(\ce{^{40}_{20}Ca^{2+}}\), not a neutral atom.

3.Three solids have these electrical behaviours: A does not conduct as a solid or melt; B conducts only when molten; C conducts in both states. Classify A, B and C.

Worked solution

A: consistent with a molecular solid (the data alone do not uniquely prove its structure). B: ionic solid; ions are fixed in the solid and mobile in the melt. C: metal; mobile electrons carry charge in both states.

4.Caesium Cs is in Group I. Give its number of valence electrons, ion, chloride formula and oxide formula.

Worked solution

Cs has one valence electron and forms \(\ce{Cs+}\) by losing it. With \(\ce{Cl-}\), the ratio is 1:1 → \(\ce{CsCl}\). With \(\ce{O^{2-}}\), two Cs⁺ balance one O²⁻ → \(\ce{Cs2O}\) (the simple oxide).

5.Which pair forms a compound with atom ratio 1:1: Mg and Cl; Na and O; K and F; Li and S? Justify using charges.

Worked solution

K and F: \(\ce{K+}\) balances \(\ce{F-}\) to give \(\ce{KF}\). The others give \(\ce{MgCl2}\) (1:2), \(\ce{Na2O}\) (2:1), and \(\ce{Li2S}\) (2:1).

6.Why does molten NaCl conduct electricity but solid NaCl does not? Does dissolving NaCl in water also allow conduction?

Worked solution

In solid NaCl the ions are held in a lattice and cannot move through the solid. In the melt they move and carry charge. Yes: aqueous NaCl contains mobile Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, so it also conducts.

Chapter 4

Organic Chemistry

Organic chemistry is the study of carbon compounds. Hydrocarbons are compounds made only of C and H atoms.

4.1Petroleum and fuels

Petroleum and natural gas formed from the remains of sea animals and plants under high temperature and pressure over millions of years. They are fossil fuels and cannot be renewed.

  • Crude oil: a mixture of many hydrocarbons (1 to 50 C atoms)
  • Natural gas: mostly methane (about 90%)
Fractional distillation
Crude oil is heated to ~380 °C in a fractionating column. The vapour rises and condenses at different heights according to boiling point: small molecules (low boiling point) come out at the top.
Cracking: breaking large molecules into smaller, more useful ones (such as petrol)\[\ce{C5H12 -> C4H8 + CH4}\]

4.4Aromatic hydrocarbons: benzene

Benzene \(\mathrm{C_6H_6}\): a colourless liquid with a sweet smell, volatile and insoluble in water (boils at 80.1 °C). All 6 C–C bonds have the same length, so it is stable: it prefers substitution to addition reactions.

Derivatives: toluene \(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH_3}\), phenol \(\mathrm{C_6H_5OH}\), aniline \(\mathrm{C_6H_5NH_2}\), styrene \(\mathrm{C_6H_5CH{=}CH_2}\).

4.5Applied chemistry: fertilisers

Plants need 3 main nutrients: nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K).

Organic fertilisers

Natural fertilisers: animal manure, leaves, compost. They improve the soil but contain little N, P and K.

Straight fertilisers

Contain only 1 nutrient. E.g. urea \(\mathrm{CO(NH_2)_2}\), \(\mathrm{(NH_4)_2SO_4}\), \(\mathrm{NH_4NO_3}\), \(\mathrm{KCl}\).

Compound fertilisers

Contain 2–3 nutrients (NP, NK, PK, NPK). E.g. \(\mathrm{KNO_3}\) (NK), \(\mathrm{(NH_4)H_2PO_4}\) (NP).
Making nitrogen fertilisers from ammonia \[\ce{2NH3 + CO2 -> CO(NH2)2 + H2O}\qquad \ce{2NH3 + H2SO4 -> (NH4)2SO4}\qquad \ce{NH3 + HNO3 -> NH4NO3}\]

Caution: using too much fertiliser lets it wash into water, polluting the environment and harming health. Use fertiliser correctly, according to what the crop needs.

1.Which process separates the components of crude oil: chemical reaction, fractional distillation, filtration, or chromatography? Explain the principle.

Worked solution

Fractional distillation. Hydrocarbons have different boiling ranges, so they vaporise and condense at different temperatures. It is a physical separation; molecules do not need to be broken apart.

2.Name the main component of natural gas. Write its molecular formula and balanced complete-combustion equation.

Worked solution

Methane, \(\ce{CH4}\). One carbon gives one CO₂; four hydrogens give two H₂O. Four oxygen atoms are needed:

\[\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}\]
3.An alkane has molar mass 58 g/mol. Find its molecular formula and write the condensed formulas and names of all its structural isomers. Use C = 12 and H = 1.

Worked solution

For an alkane, \(\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}\):

\[M=12n+(2n+2)=14n+2=58\Rightarrow n=4\]

Formula: C₄H₁₀. Two isomers: butane, CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃; 2-methylpropane, CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₃.

4.Draw the condensed structural formula of 2,2,4-trimethylpentane (isooctane). Count its carbon and hydrogen atoms to check the formula.

Worked solution

CH₃–C(CH₃)₂–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₃. Five carbons in the main chain plus three methyl carbons give 8 C. Hydrogen count: 3 + 0 + 6 + 2 + 1 + 3 + 3 = 18. Formula: C₈H₁₈, consistent with the alkane rule.

5.Write balanced complete-combustion equations for ethyne and ethene. A mixture contains 1 L of each gas. Find the oxygen volume needed and the mass of CO₂ formed. All gas volumes are at STP; use Vₘ = 22.4 L/mol, M(CO₂) = 44 g/mol.

Worked solution

\[\ce{2C2H2 + 5O2 -> 4CO2 + 2H2O}\]\[\ce{C2H4 + 3O2 -> 2CO2 + 2H2O}\]

At the same temperature and pressure, gas volumes follow mole ratios. Ethyne needs 2.5 L O₂; ethene needs 3 L O₂.

\[V(\ce{O2})=2.5+3=5.5\,\text{L}\]\[V(\ce{CO2})=2+2=4\,\text{L},\quad m=\frac{4}{22.4}\times44\approx7.86\,\text{g}\]
6.Ethyne is completely hydrogenated over a nickel catalyst. If 10 g of ethane is produced, find the volume of ethyne used at STP. Use C = 12, H = 1 and Vₘ = 22.4 L/mol.

Worked solution

\[\ce{C2H2 + 2H2 ->[Ni] C2H6}\]

\(M(\ce{C2H6})=2(12)+6=30\,\text{g/mol}\). The ethyne:ethane mole ratio is 1:1.

\[n(\ce{C2H2})=\frac{10}{30}=\frac13\,\text{mol}\]\[V=nV_m=\frac13\times22.4\approx7.47\,\text{L}\]

This assumes complete conversion to ethane and no product loss.

Fertilisers: textbook practice

Adapted from textbook printed pages 122; worked solutions added for this website.

Write your own answer first. Click a question to reveal the worked solution.

1.Which is a nitrogen fertiliser: HNO₃, N₂, NO₂ or NH₄NO₃? Give its name.

Worked solution

NH₄NO₃, ammonium nitrate. It supplies plant-available nitrogen as ammonium and nitrate ions. Nitrogen gas is not directly usable by most crops.

2.Calculate the nitrogen mass percentage in NH₄NO₃. Name the acid and base that react to form it and write the equation. Use N = 14, H = 1, O = 16.

Worked solution

\[M=2(14)+4(1)+3(16)=80\,\text{g/mol}\]\[\%N=\frac{28}{80}\times100=35\%\]

Nitric acid reacts with ammonia (a base):

\[\ce{NH3 + HNO3 -> NH4NO3}\]
3.Urea, CO(NH₂)₂, reacts with water to form ammonia and carbon dioxide. Balance the equation. Why should an equation conserve each type of atom?

Worked solution

\[\ce{CO(NH2)2 + H2O -> 2NH3 + CO2}\]

Both sides contain C:1, N:2, H:6, O:2. Chemical reactions rearrange atoms; they do not create or destroy them.

4.A river receives excessive fertiliser runoff, develops an algal bloom and then loses dissolved oxygen. Explain the chain of events and suggest one way to reduce the pollution.

Worked solution

Excess nitrates and phosphates promote algal growth. When algae die, microorganisms decompose them and consume dissolved oxygen, harming fish. Reduce runoff by applying only the needed fertiliser, avoiding application before heavy rain, or keeping vegetated buffer strips along waterways.

Summary

Atoms

\[{}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm X,\quad N=A-Z\]Shell \(n\) holds at most \(2n^2\) e⁻.

The mole

\[n=\frac{m}{M},\quad \mathcal N=6.02\times10^{23}\]

Periodic table

Group = number of valence e⁻ · period = number of shells. Group I reactivity increases down the group · Group VII decreases down the group.

Chemical bonding

Covalent: sharing e⁻ (non-metal–non-metal). Ionic: transferring e⁻ (metal–non-metal). Goal: the octet rule.

Hydrocarbons

Alkanes \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+2}\) · alkenes \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n}\) · alkynes \(\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n-2}\) · benzene \(\mathrm{C_6H_6}\)

Types of reaction

Alkanes, benzene → substitution · alkenes, alkynes → addition · all → combustion → CO₂ + H₂O

Questions and exercises

Try to solve each one yourself first, then click the question to see the answer.

1.An atom has 11 protons and 12 neutrons. Find its mass number and number of electrons, and write its symbol.
\(A=11+12=23\), \(e^-=11\) → the element sodium \(\ce{^{23}_{11}Na}\).
2.Chlorine has the isotopes \(\ce{^{35}Cl}\) (75.76%) and \(\ce{^{37}Cl}\) (24.24%). Calculate its average atomic mass.
\[35\times0.7576+37\times0.2424=26.52+8.97\approx35.5\,\text{u}\]
3.Write the electron arrangement of Mg (Z = 12). Which group and period is it in?
\((K)^2(L)^8(M)^2\) → 2 valence electrons = Group II, 3 shells = Period 3. It loses 2e⁻ to become \(\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}\).
4.Calculate the number of moles and of molecules in 36 g of water. (H = 1, O = 16)
\(M(\mathrm{H_2O})=18\,\text{g·mol}^{-1}\) ⇒ \(n=\dfrac{36}{18}=2\,\text{mol}\) ⇒ \(2\times6.02\times10^{23}=1.204\times10^{24}\) molecules.
5.Which of these compounds are ionic and which are covalent: KCl, CH₄, H₂O, LiF, SiO₂?
Ionic: KCl, LiF (metal + non-metal). Covalent: CH₄, H₂O (molecular), SiO₂ (giant covalent).
6.Write and balance the equation for the complete combustion of propane \(\mathrm{C_3H_8}\).
\[\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}\]
7.What is used to tell saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons apart?
Use bromine water (brown): alkenes/alkynes decolourise it (addition reaction), while alkanes do not. \[\ce{CH2=CH2 + Br2 -> CH2Br-CH2Br}\]
8.Why do farmers add phosphorus and potassium fertilisers as well as nitrogen fertiliser?
N helps leaves grow; P helps roots, flowers and seeds; K helps fruit and tubers and resistance to disease. Crops need all three to give a good yield.
